# Response to Reviewer 3, Comment 3

> **Comment.** The policy language is still quite limited. The system focuses on fixed-length
> Boolean RPN programs, while many realistic healthcare consent policies involve temporal
> constraints, revocation, obligations, delegation, purpose limitation, institutional approval, and
> evolving consent. Some of these are treated as external predicate bits, but this moves complexity
> outside the proven statement. The paper should better explain what kinds of real consent policies
> can actually be supported end-to-end.

**Response.** We thank the reviewer for identifying an important ambiguity in the submitted
description. We agree that describing a condition merely as an “external predicate” does not give
an end-to-end authorization guarantee: it leaves open whether its semantics, issuer, scope, and
validity are cryptographically connected to the hidden policy and access request. We therefore both
narrow the claim and extend the prototype in a focused way.

The revised system is not a general-purpose consent-language engine.
It supports a restricted **access-time consent** profile:
a provider can combine a bounded number of categorical conditions with Boolean logic, bind a private declared purpose to a request, require a policy-selected institutional approval credential, constrain both policy and approval to validity windows, and replace a policy version so that resources explicitly rebound to the successor immediately cease to honour earlier grants.
The proof establishes these properties at authorization time. It does not claim to observe how data are used after disclosure or to implement every lifecycle feature of healthcare consent.

## 1. What is inside the proved statement

V2 retains the fixed-length RPN interpreter, but the institutional-approval predicate is no longer
an unconstrained Boolean bit. Its public statement is:

```
pubSignals = [isAuthorized, policyCommitment, paramsCommitment, contextHash, requestEpoch]
```

`requestEpoch = floor(block.timestamp / 3600)` is the current one-hour EVM bucket. The requester
pseudonym, resource identifier, nonce, declared purpose, approval identifier, and validity ranges
are private witness values.
`contextHash = Poseidon(idReq, idRes, nonce)` binds the proof to the requester, resource, and session without publishing those values.

For `INSTITUTIONAL_APPROVAL_V1`, the circuit recomputes the issuer-signed message:

```
Poseidon(schemaTag, policyCommitment, idReq, idRes, purposeCode,
         validFromEpoch, validUntilEpoch, approvalId)
```

It verifies the issuer's EdDSA signature, checks that the issuer public key and schema tag are
committed policy parameters, and enforces `validFromEpoch <= requestEpoch <= validUntilEpoch`.
The hidden policy's own validity window is committed in the same parameter commitment and checked
against `requestEpoch`. The contract rejects a proof unless its epoch equals its current hour.
Consequently, the approval cannot be reused for a different requester, resource, purpose, policy,
issuer, approval identifier, or validity interval.

This is deliberately narrower than converting every real-world condition into ZK constraints: it
brings access-time eligibility into the proved relation while retaining a bounded, reusable circuit
and policy-independent public interface.

## 2. End-to-end support and explicit boundary

| Consent feature | V2 status | What is established |
|---|---|---|
| Categorical DUO-like, disease-scope, and requester-attribute conditions | Supported within fixed bounds | A fixed `N=6`, `M=5`, `DEPTH=20` Boolean RPN policy is well formed, committed, and satisfied. Ontology conditions use committed whitelist roots. |
| Purpose limitation | Supported at access time | The private `purposeCode` is included in the issuer-signed approval digest and never appears in calldata, events, or public signals. |
| Institutional/ethics approval | Supported for `INSTITUTIONAL_APPROVAL_V1` | Issuer, schema, requester, resource, purpose, policy commitment, approval ID, and validity range are signature-bound and checked in the circuit. |
| Temporal validity | Supported at access time | Hidden policy and approval windows are committed and compared with the strict current one-hour chain epoch. A boundary-crossing request must be reproved. |
| Consent withdrawal / policy evolution | Supported through explicit replacement and resource rebind | A provider creates a direct successor and explicitly rebinds a resource. Historical grants remain auditable, but are immediately non-current. |
| Individual approval-credential revocation | Not supported | The signature and expiry are proved, but no credential-revocation registry is checked. |
| Obligations and post-access controls | Not supported | The proof shows approved purpose at access time; it cannot prove actual downstream use, deletion, reporting, or other later conduct. |
| Delegation and re-delegation | Not supported | No delegated-authority chain, attenuation, depth bound, or revocation semantics is represented. |
| General evolving/dynamic consent | Partially supported | Replacement/rebinding changes policy prospectively; continuous preference updates, automatic propagation, and withdrawal of already disclosed data are outside scope. |

The intended end-to-end use case is therefore: a requester with required private attributes seeks a
resource for a private declared purpose, during the policy interval, and presents an unexpired
approval specifically covering that requester, resource, purpose, and policy. The system proves
that statement without exposing the policy, requester pseudonym, resource identifier, purpose, or
approval contents through the proof's public inputs.

## 3. Why the remaining boundary is intentional

Post-access obligations are not simply predicates evaluated at request time. “Do not redistribute,”
“delete after 30 days,” and “report a publication,” for example, require observation or trusted
attestation of future off-chain conduct. Similarly, a general delegation language needs authority
chains, scope attenuation, loop prevention, and revocation semantics. Treating these as one-time
bits would overstate the proof guarantee.

The revision will thus distinguish:

1. **In-circuit, policy-bound checks:** typed institutional approval, its scope/issuer/validity,
   committed ontology membership, and policy validity.
2. **On-chain access-state checks:** current epoch, active policy/resource state, direct-successor
   relation, and whether a resource is still bound to the policy that produced the record.
3. **Out-of-scope lifecycle controls:** credential revocation, delegation, post-disclosure
   obligations, actual-use monitoring, and retraction of data already released.

The approval issuer remains trusted for the truthfulness of its approval, but the scope of what it
issued is now checked cryptographically rather than supplied as an arbitrary predicate outcome.

## 4. Prototype validation

- `test/v2_typed_approval.test.js` produces a valid approval witness and confirms that changing the
  purpose, schema tag, or predicate type prevents witness generation. The signed digest also binds
  requester, resource, policy commitment, issuer key, approval ID, and validity interval.
- `test/v2_consent_lifecycle.test.js` confirms stale-epoch rejection, a current-hour authorization,
  immediate invalidation after rebind while preserving the historical record, and direct-successor /
  resource-provider checks.
- The regenerated circuit has 129,846 constraints (80,947 non-linear and 48,899 linear) and five
  total public signals, including its output. A locally generated Groth16 proof verifies against the
  regenerated five-input verifier.

These are prototype and local-test results, not a substitute for the revised manuscript's full
performance evaluation or a production ceremony.
