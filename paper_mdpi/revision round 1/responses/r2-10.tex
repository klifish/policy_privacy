	\RevisionLocation{Section ``Background,'' subsection ``Cryptographic Access Control in Blockchain Healthcare,'' first paragraph.}
	\begin{revisiontext}
		Fine-grained consent management in blockchain-based healthcare data sharing is commonly built on cryptographic access-control primitives, chiefly Ciphertext-Policy Attribute-Based Encryption (CP-ABE) and Proxy Re-Encryption (PRE).
		In CP-ABE, the encrypting party embeds an attribute-based access policy into the ciphertext, so that only requesters whose credentials satisfy the policy can decrypt the protected data.
		PRE lets a semi-trusted intermediary transform a ciphertext encrypted for one party into one decryptable by a designated requester, delegating access without exposing the plaintext.
		Both primitives remain the building blocks of current blockchain healthcare designs: recent schemes combine PRE with on-chain records to delegate access to medical records and IoMT data, and CP-ABE variants hide the access policy and attributes, in one case also replacing the single trusted authority with distributed key generation.
		These primitives establish who may obtain the data; how they relate to \emph{policy} confidentiality on a public chain is discussed in Section~3.
	\end{revisiontext}

	\RevisionLocation{Section ``Related Work,'' paragraph on hidden-policy access control.}
	\begin{revisiontext}
		Hidden-policy access-control work reduces direct policy exposure: CP-ABE variants hide the access structure in the ciphertext.
		Wu et al. instead encrypt policy and attribute values under an additively homomorphic cryptosystem.
		A smart contract computes their encrypted difference, one assistant node per organization decrypts it partially with a zero-knowledge proof of correct decryption, and the contract decides without seeing the values; every request therefore needs every organization online.
		Functional encryption gives a more general cryptographic abstraction for revealing function outputs while hiding inputs, but it does not by itself yield publicly verifiable authorization evidence.
		Private-state blockchain systems, including Secret Network and Oasis/Ekiden, protect smart-contract state or execution through encrypted state and trusted execution environments.
		These lines of work address adjacent forms of policy or state hiding, but TPS-ZKPI targets a more specific setting: hiding consent policies on a public chain while retaining public verifiability of each authorization decision.
		Even where policies are hidden, existing designs may still leak policy-dependent artifacts through ciphertext structures, policy-update transactions, authorization workflows, or repeated interactions.
		In the homomorphic design, for example, the Boolean structure of the policy and its attribute names stay on the ledger, and the per-attribute match results can be recomputed from the ciphertexts and partial decryptions recorded with each decision.
		Moreover, many target permissioned or off-chain settings, such as that design's coalition blockchain, where enforcement artifacts are not globally visible as they are on public blockchains.
	\end{revisiontext}

	\RevisionLocation{Section ``Related Work,'' new paragraph on self-sovereign-identity architectures.}
	\begin{revisiontext}
		Self-sovereign-identity (SSI) architectures move authorization to verifiable claims held by the requester and shift the question to whom the holder may trust with those claims.
		Buccafurri et al. combine smart contracts with ciphertext-policy attribute-based proxy re-encryption so that a holder's claims can be read only by verifiers that satisfy a holder-defined policy, with the contract keeping verifiers accountable for the access they obtain; zero-knowledge presentations of credentials likewise let a patient authenticate anonymously against public or private chains, and surveys of zero-knowledge proofs for blockchain privacy catalogue this design space.
		TPS-ZKPI sits on the other side of the same authorization relation.
		In SSI designs the protected object is the requester's claim set, governed by the holder's policy; here it is the provider's consent policy.
		The requester's claims are consumed as private witnesses of a proof whose statement is fixed and whose verifier is a public contract.
		Any observer may therefore verify the proof, and accountability rests on the recorded, context-bound outcome rather than on a contract that meters the release of claims.
		The trust the two settings place off-chain also differs: an SSI verifier that decrypts a claim learns it, whereas the TPS learns both policy and claims, which is why Section~4 treats the TPS as an explicit trust boundary and Table~3 states what survives its compromise.
		Combining the two---holder-authorized presentation of the claims that TPS-ZKPI consumes as witnesses, including a per-request signature over the authorization context---would prevent a TPS that observes one presentation from reusing the evidence for fresh sessions, as discussed in Section~7.
	\end{revisiontext}
