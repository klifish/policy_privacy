\begin{reviewcomment}{9}
	The discussion and conclusion summarize the proposed idea. However, there is a repetition of the same conclusion in the abstract, discussion, and conclusion. This repetition of the same information should be avoided.
\end{reviewcomment}

\begin{reviewresponse}{9}
	The three texts now serve different purposes.
	The abstract states the result and the overhead figures once.
	The Discussion interprets the results---the scope of the claim, the two criterion failures and their origin, the cost of padding, the trust model, and the attribute-origin guarantee---without restating the abstract.
	The Conclusion avoids repeating the abstract's task counts and overhead figures; it summarizes the contribution, the meaning of the results, the limitations, and the directions that follow.

	\textbf{Changes in the manuscript.}
	Representative passages showing the distinct role of each section are reproduced below.

	\RevisionLocation{Abstract.}
	\begin{revisiontext}
		In blockchain-based healthcare data sharing, consent policies are expected to be enforced verifiably and auditably, but public-chain transparency may expose sensitive policy information through on-chain representations, verification interfaces, and execution artifacts.
		Existing privacy-preserving solutions mainly protect data contents, identities, or transactions, not the governing consent policies.
		We propose the Trusted Proof Service-assisted Zero-Knowledge Policy Interpreter (TPS-ZKPI), which lets an on-chain verifier check authorization decisions while limiting leakage about policy content, identity, and structure.
		TPS-ZKPI combines fixed-length policy encoding, a reusable zero-knowledge policy interpreter, commitment-based policy binding, and public-chain transcript normalization.
		We evaluate TPS-ZKPI on Polygon Amoy against metadata-only public-chain adversaries with supervised and unsupervised inference attacks. Of $45$ supervised tasks, $43$ remain below the multi-seed leakage criterion; diagnostic analysis traces the two threshold crossings, and one corresponding small clustering result, to finite-corpus coincidences in policy-independent padding targets. A paired ablation attributes suppression of the modeled gas channel to randomized padding, at an overhead of about $46\%$ on access and $38\%$ on commit gas.
	\end{revisiontext}

	\RevisionLocation{Section ``Discussion,'' opening and scope paragraphs.}
	\begin{revisiontext}
		The evaluation supports a scoped claim: against the modeled public-chain metadata adversary, TPS-ZKPI provides empirical resistance to policy leakage.
		Of the $45$ supervised tasks, $43$ stay below the multi-seed leakage criterion, and unsupervised clustering shows no recoverable structure beyond one small, permutation-significant nesting-depth ARI.
		The two tasks that meet the criterion---one ICD-10 chapter and the \texttt{nestingDepth} \texttt{medium}/\texttt{deep} contrast---are reported as failures, and both are carried by the random pad target rather than by the policy: subtracting the logged target from the access gas returns each to PASS, and replacing the gas features by policy-independent draws reproduces such failures in four of ten replicates.
		The scope of this claim is the adversary of Section~6.1: a passive observer of commitments, proofs, public parameters, calldata, events, and gas, on a corpus of generated policies.
		Timing and ordering of transactions, mempool and broadcast behaviour, off-chain communication with the TPS, adaptive requester probing, repeated access attempts, correlations between authorization outcomes and policy classes (every request in the corpus is granted), and compromised TPS behaviour are not modeled, and the results should not be read as establishing general policy confidentiality against them.
	\end{revisiontext}

	\RevisionLocation{Section ``Conclusion,'' contribution and future-directions paragraphs.}
	\begin{revisiontext}
		This work presents TPS-ZKPI, a Trusted Proof Service-assisted zero-knowledge approach for policy-private consent enforcement in blockchain-based healthcare data sharing. TPS-ZKPI commits hidden provider-defined policies, evaluates bounded Boolean policies through a reusable interpreter, and normalizes the public verification transcript to reduce metadata-level leakage. Its authorization relation binds each proof to the committed policy and request context, while profile-backed attributes derive in-circuit from issuer-signed credentials under an explicit issuer-governance assumption.

		The next steps are therefore clear: reduced-trust proof generation and holder-signed request contexts can reduce the TPS trust concentration; governed issuer registries with in-circuit membership and revocation witnesses can strengthen credential provenance; and evaluation against active adversaries can test transcript normalization in the timing and ordering channels beyond the present model.
	\end{revisiontext}
	\end{reviewresponse}
