# Response to Reviewer 1, Comment 3

> **Comment.** The problem of attribute-origin requires further investigation. The issuer-signature mechanism is a placeholder for consistency, based on Poseidon, and thus does not provide cryptographic unforgeability as it stands. Thus, the proof proves the consistency of the given witness but does not prove that the private attributes are derived from an authorized issuer. This difference is particularly important in a healthcare authorization system. **The authors should clarify which authorization properties are guaranteed by the prototype they implemented, and which are dependent on external trust.** **Even an analytical assessment of a concrete design for the integration of real credential verification would greatly improve the work.**

**Response.** We thank the reviewer for emphasizing this distinction. We agree that witness consistency alone is insufficient to establish attribute origin, and that a healthcare authorization prototype must separate cryptographic credential verification from trust in the real-world issuer.

During the revision, we clarified and documented the implemented in-circuit EdDSA-Poseidon verification path for a credential carrying fixed DUO-relevant claims. Its seven issuer-signed claim bits represent research use, health or medical use, methods development, genetic study, ancestry, commercial use, and non-profit use. The signed message begins with a fixed credential schema/domain tag and binds the policy commitment, requester pseudonym, protected resource, these claims, validity interval, and credential identifier. For a policy that activates this holder-bound credential, the requester pseudonym is derived as Poseidon of a private holder secret, binding the credential to that pseudonym. Before proof generation, an issuer signs this message with its private key, and the credential is supplied to the TPS as private off-chain evidence. The issuer key is bound to the policy parameters, and the circuit derives the DUO terms GRU, HMB, NMDS, GSO, NPUNCU, NPOA, NPU, and NCU from the signed claims. Under the unforgeability of EdDSA for correctly generated Baby Jubjub keys and the collision resistance of Poseidon, a TPS cannot alter these signed values and still produce an accepting proof.

The guarantee remains deliberately scoped. The prototype proves signature validity under a provider-selected, policy-bound issuer key; it does not establish that the key belongs to an accredited healthcare institution or that the issuer truthfully issued the credential. Ontology predicates prove membership in provider-committed code sets, and requester allowlists prove inclusion of a holder pseudonym in provider-committed lists, but neither independently authenticates every real-world fact. Issuer-key governance, credential revocation, real-world identity verification, and protection against a compromised TPS remain external trust and lifecycle functions. For reproducibility, the evaluation generator uses a deterministic test issuer to create the private profile-credential witnesses; this exercises the cryptographic path but is not production institutional identity or key management.

The revised Discussion presents a concrete production design: a governed issuer registry with schema-qualified, time-bounded keys, canonical and subgroup-safe key validation, and proof of possession; a domain-separated credential containing a holder-key commitment, the seven claims for research, health or medical, methods-development, genetic, ancestry, commercial, and non-profit use, scope, validity, and identifier; in-circuit verification of the credential signature and private issuer-registry membership; a holder-generated signature over each authorization context; and an epoch-specific revocation accumulator or sparse-Merkle root. Fixed-format state commitments can incorporate the registry and revocation roots without making the public interface policy dependent. Existing credentials using non-circuit-friendly signatures would either require a more expensive in-circuit verifier or an additional SNARK-friendly credential issued by the original issuer; TPS re-signing would not preserve issuer-origin assurance.

The targeted DUO V3 tests accept a valid credential and reject a rogue issuer, a forged signature, invalid validity intervals, substitutions of every bound field, inconsistent claim-derived attributes, and invalid holder binding. All 25 targeted tests pass, including full Groth16 proof and on-chain grant/deny cases, and the frozen RC1 release artifacts validate successfully. The final RC1 cost profile already contains this credential verifier, so we report the absolute system cost without claiming an unsupported isolated delta.

The manuscript changes are located in the Problem and Threat Model, the new Design subsection “Credential-Origin Binding,” and the Discussion.
